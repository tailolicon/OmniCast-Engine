TITLE: The Manifest at Clinic A
HOOK: The car behind me stopped being traffic when it turned into the second clinic with its lights off.
NARRATION:
I was thirty-five when I took the extra overnight courier work, mostly because I wanted the van paid off before something expensive failed on it. My route covered three small clinics out in the county. They were all closed by the time I reached them, so I worked alone from locked drop box to locked drop box with a cooler on the passenger seat and my phone in the cup holder.

The route was simple enough that I knew where every turn came before the sign appeared. Clinic A sat behind a feed store near the edge of town. Clinic B was farther out, by itself on a county road with a gravel lot and one light over the back entrance. Clinic C brought me close to the highway again. I collected or delivered whatever the paperwork called for, checked the seals, signed the sheet, and moved on.

Clinic A was where the night went wrong.

The back door there was supposed to stay locked during overnight drops. We never used it. The box was mounted on the wall beside it, and the procedure was clear about not entering the building after hours. That night the door was open by about the width of my hand.

I stopped before I reached the box. The cooler was tucked against my hip, and my keys were already in my other hand. I could see the thin black line between the door and the frame. The hall light inside was on. Through the gap I could make out a strip of tile and the corner of the counter where staff left paperwork during the day.

I did not go inside.

I set the cooler down beside the box and listened for a moment. The building had the normal low hum I knew from daytime pickups. No voices. No footsteps. Nothing moved past the slice of hallway I could see.

The door bothered me because that clinic was strict about it. A nurse had corrected me once for standing too close to it while I waited for someone to finish a daytime handoff. After closing, the whole point of the outside box was that nobody on my route needed the door.

I pulled the box open and did my part of the exchange. The route sheet had been left on the counter inside where I could see it through the gap. It was spread open instead of clipped shut. I could see names and handling notes in rows, though I wasn't standing there trying to read them. One name did catch my eye because the cooler I had for Clinic B was tied to it, and the medication listed beside the name affected how the specimen had been collected.

That kind of paperwork was supposed to be put away.

I stepped back from the door and called the after-hours number printed on my route sheet. It rang to voicemail. I said the back door at Clinic A was standing open, gave my name, and said I was leaving without touching it.

I waited a few seconds in case somebody inside had heard me talking. Nobody came to the door.

I completed the drop, took the receipt I was supposed to take, and put everything back in the van. I locked the doors as soon as I sat down.

For a while I watched Clinic A in the mirror. The back door stayed where it was. No person came out to close it. No car started in the lot.

I pulled away.

The road passed a few houses before the fields opened up. There were porch lights in the distance and dark gaps between them. I had driven that stretch enough that I knew which houses usually had somebody awake late and which ones went black early.

For the first part of the drive, there were no headlights behind me.

A sedan came up after the last cluster of houses.

That wasn't strange by itself. People used the county road to cut toward the highway, and sometimes they sat behind my van because the passing spots were bad. I kept my usual pace and expected it to either pass when the road opened or fall back when I got farther out.

It did neither.

When I slowed into a curve, the sedan slowed. When I came out and picked up speed, it matched me again. The distance between us stayed so even that I started using roadside mailboxes to judge it.

I moved a little slower on the next straight stretch.

The sedan moved slower too.

I eased back up.

It did the same.

That went on for three empty miles.

There was no other traffic. I could see the sedan's headlights and the dark shape of its hood, but the glare kept me from seeing the driver. It never crowded my bumper. That almost made it worse, because whoever was driving seemed comfortable staying where I could not get a good look at them.

I started checking for places where I could pull in if I needed help. Most of the farmhouses were too far from the road. A few had long gravel drives with gates. I did not want to turn into a stranger's property with another car behind me unless I had no other choice.

I considered taking the next road instead of going to Clinic B. That would have put me farther from the pharmacy hub, and I still did not know whether the car was actually following me. I kept to the route.

The turn for Clinic B came up on my right.

I left my signal off until I was nearly there.

The sedan was still behind me.

I signaled and turned.

It turned with me.

That was the first point where I stopped treating it like normal traffic.

I reached for my phone and tried to call dispatch. The screen showed no signal.

Clinic B was bad for that. Everybody who drove the route knew it. The lot had no live staff overnight and no cell signal, and the nearest occupied building was a pharmacy hub several minutes down the highway.

There was no receptionist inside. There was no guard. There was not even another business sharing the lot.

The van was my only shelter.

I kept it moving instead of pulling into my usual space. The sedan followed me past the front of the clinic and into the gravel area behind it. The one light over the back entrance made a pale circle on the wall and left most of the lot dark.

I made a slow loop with the van.

The sedan did not loop with me. It rolled into a space near the back entrance and stopped.

For a second I thought the driver might have business there too. Then the headlights went out.

The engine kept running.

That changed the whole lot for me. With the lights off, the sedan became a dark shape under the edge of the clinic light. I could see a faint glow from its dash through the windshield. I could not see a face.

I drove to the far side of the gravel and stopped with the van pointed toward the road. I left the engine on. Normally I would park closer to the drop box, take the cooler out, scan the paperwork, and be gone before the van had fully cooled down.

I did not touch the cooler.

I tried my phone again.

Still no service.

I had already called the after-hours number about Clinic A. At Clinic B there was nobody live to report anything to, and my phone could not reach dispatch or police. The only practical place I could get help was the pharmacy hub by the highway.

I knew I could drive there immediately.

I also knew that the Clinic B cooler was time-sensitive, and abandoning the stop without reporting why would start its own chain of calls. That did not matter more than being safe. What slowed me down was that I still had only a car sitting in a parking lot with its lights off.

I watched it through the windshield.

My hand stayed on the gear selector.

The engine in the sedan idled steadily. I could see a little vibration in the reflected light on its windshield. Nobody inside waved or flashed the lights.

A minute felt long in that lot.

I decided I was leaving.

Before I could move, the sedan's driver's door opened.

A man got out and stood beside the car.

I couldn't make out much in that light. He looked average height, maybe heavier through the shoulders because of his jacket. I could not see his hair clearly. He shut the sedan door and looked straight at my van.

He did not head for the clinic.

He started walking toward me.

That settled it.

I put the transmission in drive and rolled toward the exit.

He raised one hand, palm out, like he wanted me to wait. I kept moving. He changed direction and walked faster toward the driver's side instead of staying in front of me.

I had the window closed. He said something I could not make out.

I should have kept going.

Instead I stopped with several feet between us and cracked the window just enough to hear him. I wanted to know whether he was going to identify himself as staff, a driver, or somebody who had a reason to be there. That choice still bothers me because I had already decided to leave.

He said a patient's last name.

I did not answer.

He came another step closer.

He said the name again, then added, "I need that cooler early. They said you'd have it."

I looked at the cooler on the passenger seat.

There were handling labels on the outer sleeve, but the patient's name he had said was not visible from where he stood. I had checked that label before leaving the previous stop. I knew which side faced the seat.

"Who are you?" I asked.

He ignored the question.

He leaned slightly toward the window and looked past me at the passenger seat.

"It's the one for her," he said.

Then he named the medication written beside that patient's entry on the manifest at Clinic A.

That was when I went completely still.

One hand was on the steering wheel. The other rested by the window switch. My eyes went from his face to the cooler and then down to the lock button. I remember my thumb pressing into the edge of the switch hard enough to hurt.

For half a second I thought he could be somebody from the clinic who had been told about the delivery. Then he said, "You saw the sheet back there. It's the same name."

I had not told him I had seen any sheet.

The sheet had been inside Clinic A.

He was outside my van at Clinic B.

I asked him how he knew the name.

He did not answer that either.

He pointed through the window toward the cooler.

"Just hand it out," he said. "You don't have to get out."

His voice was calm. He was not shouting or threatening me. That made it easier to hear exactly what he was saying, and what he was saying was wrong.

I looked down at the cooler again.

He had not asked for whatever I was carrying. He had not guessed that I had medical material because of the van or the clinic. He had named a particular patient, and he had named the medication written on the open manifest beside that patient. He knew which delivery he wanted me to hand over.

That meant he had already read the manifest.

I hit the lock button.

His eyes dropped toward the sound.

I raised the window.

He put his hand flat against the glass once, not hard. Then he moved closer to the door.

I put my foot on the accelerator.

He knocked on the window with two fingers.

"Wait," he said.

I shook my head.

The window was nearly closed when he reached for the frame beside the mirror. His fingers came around the edge as the last gap disappeared.

I started moving.

He walked with the van.

Because I had the wheel turned toward the exit, the driver's side passed closer to him before I could straighten out. I remember watching the gravel ahead and his hand in the edge of my vision at the same time.

He grabbed the driver's door handle.

The handle lifted.

The door stayed shut because I had locked it.

He pulled again.

I pressed the accelerator harder.

For a moment his hand stayed on the handle as the van moved. He had to take quick steps to keep up. Then his grip slipped.

I thought I was past him.

The turn carried the passenger side toward the open part of the lot, and he moved with the van instead of backing away. He reached across the space toward the passenger door.

The cooler was still on the seat.

I understood what he was doing before his hand got anywhere near it.

I leaned across and shoved the cooler onto the floor.

The van drifted toward the edge of the gravel because I had taken one hand off the wheel. I corrected too hard. The tires moved onto the softer edge, then bit again when I got back onto the packed part of the lot.

I looked up.

The man was behind me now.

He did not run after the van on foot.

He turned back toward the sedan.

That scared me more than if he had chased for a few steps. He had another way to follow me.

I did not wait to see him get inside.

The exit from Clinic B fed onto a narrow road before it reached the highway. I turned onto it and drove toward the pharmacy hub.

My phone still had no signal.

I tried anyway. The call screen sat there and failed.

The first stretch had drainage ditches on both sides and almost no shoulder. I kept my eyes forward because one bad move would have put the van where I could not drive it out. Every few seconds I checked the mirror.

At first there were no headlights.

Then I saw a glow come around the bend behind me.

I could not tell whether it belonged to the sedan. The road was too dark and the distance was too great. I did not slow down to find out.

I had a very simple plan by then. Get to the highway. Get to the pharmacy hub. Get under the lights. Call 911 as soon as my phone came back.

There was nothing else to work through.

The van felt small in a way it never had before. Usually I liked having everything within reach. Cooler on the passenger side. Paperwork in the center tray. Phone by my hand. That night all I could think about was the driver's handle lifting while I was sitting inches away from it.

I checked the lock again even though I knew it was locked.

The headlights behind me stayed visible for another stretch.

They did not gain quickly. They did not fall away either.

I had liked how empty that route felt right up until emptiness meant there was nobody close enough to help.

The highway sign appeared ahead.

My phone found a weak signal, lost it, then picked it up again.

I did not call while I was still on the narrow road. I wanted both hands on the wheel until I reached the highway.

The pharmacy hub came into view after the turn. It was a low building with a loading area on one side and bright lights across the front. Trucks came and went through the night, and there were usually employees inside even when the public entrance was locked.

I turned into the lot.

The headlights behind me continued straight along the highway.

I did not know if that car was the sedan.

I drove all the way to the front of the pharmacy hub and parked beside the lit entrance. I left the engine running. I locked the van again before I touched anything else.

My phone showed service.

I called 911 from inside the locked van.

I told the dispatcher I was a medical courier. I said a man had followed me into an unstaffed clinic lot, asked for a specific delivery by patient name, and pulled my driver's door handle when I tried to leave. I described the sedan as best I could. I had not seen a plate.

The dispatcher asked whether the man had a weapon.

I said I had not seen one.

She asked whether he had gotten into the van.

I said no.

She asked whether I was somewhere occupied now.

I looked through the pharmacy windows and said yes.

She told me to stay where I was and keep the doors locked.

I called my supervisor after that. The call was short. I said I was safe at the pharmacy hub and that I had not completed the Clinic B drop. I said police were on the way.

My supervisor told me to leave the cooler sealed and stay put.

I stayed in the van.

There were people moving behind the glass doors of the pharmacy hub. One person passed through the lobby carrying a stack of flattened boxes. Another stopped near the entrance and looked toward my van, then kept walking. I remember that because it was the first ordinary thing I had seen since Clinic A.

I watched every car that turned into the lot.

None looked like the sedan to me.

A deputy arrived and parked beside the van. I did not unlock my door until he came to the window and identified himself.

I got out with my phone in my hand.

He took my report there under the lights.

I started at Clinic A because I did not know how else to explain the patient name. I told him the back door had been propped open. I told him the manifest was visible through the gap. I told him I had called the clinic's after-hours number and left a message before I drove away.

Then I described the county road.

I explained how the sedan had held the same distance behind me for several miles. I explained that it turned when I turned into Clinic B. I described the headlights going out while the engine stayed running.

Then I told him about the man.

I repeated the patient's name for the deputy and explained why hearing it bothered me. I told him the name was not visible on the cooler from where the man stood. I told him the man had also named the medication that was written on the manifest at Clinic A.

The deputy asked whether the man had shown identification.

I said no.

He asked whether the man had claimed to work for either clinic.

I said he had not.

He asked whether I had seen him at Clinic A.

I said no.

He asked whether anything had been taken.

I opened the passenger door while the deputy stood beside me. The cooler was still on the floor where I had shoved it. The seal was intact.

I said nothing had been taken.

The deputy wrote all of it down.

He asked for the sedan description again. I gave him the color as I remembered it and the general shape. I could not give him much more than that because I had mostly seen headlights in my mirror and a dark car in a badly lit lot.

He checked what I could provide and said no vehicle matching that description was registered nearby.

He did not say that proved anything. I had no plate, no second witness, and nothing that could show the sedan he checked was the same one I had seen.

He finished taking my report at the pharmacy hub.

That was the police report. Nobody found the man for me that night, and nobody produced anything that confirmed what I had seen.

I stayed under the pharmacy lights until my supervisor arranged for another courier to take the sealed cooler. I did not drive back toward Clinic B.

The next part came from my own paperwork, not from police or clinic staff.

While I was filling out the incident form, I wrote down the words the man had used so I would not forget them. I got to the part where he said I had seen the sheet back there, and I stopped writing for a moment.

The patient name he used was on a manifest sheet left open at the first clinic, meaning he had been inside before I ever arrived.

There was no other way for his sentence to make sense to me. He did not merely know a patient at Clinic B. He knew what I had seen through the open door at Clinic A. He knew the medication written beside the name. He knew which cooler on my route was connected to it.

I kept thinking about the order of it.

The back door was already open when I arrived. The manifest was already spread where it could be read. The sedan did not appear until after I left Clinic A. Then it stayed behind me all the way to the road for Clinic B.

At Clinic B, the man did not ask whether I was the courier.

He did not ask whether I had a delivery.

He asked for the cooler as if my arrival there had been expected.

That was the part I could not turn into a normal mistake between two clinics. A staff member expecting a delivery could have identified himself. A family member who knew a patient could not have known what I had seen on an internal route sheet. A random person following a van would not know which name on a manifest mattered.

I had no proof beyond what happened to me, so I stopped trying to make it cleaner than it was.

For a while I took daytime runs instead. When I started covering overnight again, I changed one part of my routine. I stopped sitting in dark clinic lots to finish paperwork or make calls. If a stop felt wrong, I drove to the pharmacy hub and did those things under the lights.

I still carry the cooler on the passenger side because that is the safest place to keep it secured while I drive. I do not leave it high on the seat anymore when I am parked. It goes on the floor before I unlock anything.

The Clinic B lot looks ordinary whenever I pass it now. Same gravel. Same light above the back entrance. Same dark road leading away from it.

What I remember is the handle lifting while the cooler sat beside me, and the calm way he named something he should never have been able to read.

My van still ends those overnight runs under the pharmacy lights, because no one ever explained how he knew my route stopped at Clinic B before I got there, or why the manifest at Clinic A identified the exact cooler beside me that night.
