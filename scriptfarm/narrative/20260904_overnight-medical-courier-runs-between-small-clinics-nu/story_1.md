TITLE: The Manifest at Clinic A
HOOK: The car behind me stopped being traffic when it turned into the second clinic with its lights off.
NARRATION:
I was thirty-five when I took the extra overnight courier work, mostly because I wanted the van paid off before anything expensive broke on it. My route covered three small clinics out in the county. They were all closed by the time I reached them, so I worked alone from locked drop box to locked drop box with a cooler on the passenger seat and my phone in the cup holder.

The route was simple enough that I could do it without thinking much about it. Clinic A sat behind a feed store near the edge of town. Clinic B was farther out, by itself on a county road with a gravel lot and one light over the back entrance. Clinic C was closer to the highway again. I collected or delivered whatever the paperwork called for, checked the seal, signed the sheet, and moved on.

Clinic A was the stop where the night went wrong.

The back door there was supposed to stay locked during overnight drops. We never used it. The box was mounted on the wall beside it, and the procedure was clear about not entering the building after hours. That night the door was open by about the width of my hand.

I stood there with the cooler against my hip and looked at the black line between the door and the frame. The hall light inside was on. I could see a slice of tile and the corner of the counter where staff left paperwork during the day.

I didn't go inside.

I set the cooler down, pulled the drop box open, and did my part of the exchange. The sheet for my route had been left on the counter where I could see it through the gap. It was spread open instead of clipped shut. That was sloppy, but clinic staff were always rushing at closing time. The open door bothered me more.

I called the after-hours number printed on my route sheet. It rang to voicemail. I said the back door at Clinic A was standing open, gave my name, and said I was leaving without touching it. Then I took a picture of nothing more than my own signed drop receipt for work, put the cooler in the van, and locked myself in.

For the first few minutes after I pulled away, I kept checking the mirror. There were no headlights behind me. The road passed a few houses and then opened into fields, with long dark stretches between driveways.

A sedan came up behind me after the last cluster of houses.

That wasn't strange by itself. People used that road to cut toward the highway. I kept my normal speed and expected it to either pass or fall back.

It did neither.

When I slowed for a curve, it slowed. When the road straightened and I picked up speed again, it stayed in the same place in my mirror. Not close enough to look aggressive. Not far enough to disappear.

That went on for three empty miles.

There was no other traffic. I could see the sedan's headlights, the dark shape of its hood, and nothing about the driver. I started looking for a farmhouse with lights on, but the ones I passed were set way back from the road. I didn't want to pull into somebody's driveway with a stranger behind me unless I had to.

The road to Clinic B came up on my right. I signaled late on purpose, hoping the sedan would continue straight.

It turned with me.

That was the first point where I stopped treating it like normal traffic.

I reached for my phone and tried to call dispatch. The screen showed no signal. Clinic B was bad for that. Everybody who drove the route knew it. The lot had no live staff overnight and no cell signal, and the nearest occupied building was a pharmacy hub several minutes down the highway.

I had nowhere useful to go on foot.

I kept the van moving through the lot instead of parking in my usual spot. The sedan followed me past the front of the clinic. I made a slow loop around the gravel, watching it through the side mirror.

For a second I thought the driver might have business there too. Then the sedan rolled into a space near the back entrance and shut its headlights off.

The engine kept running.

I could see a faint shine from the dash through the windshield. The car was pointed toward the exit. Nobody got out.

I drove to the far side of the lot and stopped with the van facing the road. I left the engine on. My drop at Clinic B normally took less than a minute, but I had no reason to get out while that car sat there.

I tried my phone again. Still no service.

I thought about driving straight to the pharmacy hub and coming back later. The problem was the cooler for Clinic B was time-sensitive enough that skipping the stop would trigger calls from the lab. That was not worth risking myself over, but I was still trying to decide whether I was actually in danger or only dealing with someone being weird in a parking lot.

I watched the sedan for a while.

Nothing moved.

Then its driver's door opened.

A man got out and stood beside the car. I couldn't make out much in that light. He looked average height, maybe a little heavier through the shoulders because of his jacket. He did not wave. He shut his door and started walking toward my van.

That settled it for me.

I put the transmission in drive and moved toward the exit.

He raised one hand then, palm out, like he wanted me to wait. I kept rolling. He changed direction and walked faster toward the driver's side instead of the front of the van.

I cracked my window only enough to hear him. I should not have done even that, but at the time I wanted to know what he was going to say before I left.

He said a patient's last name.

I didn't answer.

He said it again, then added, "I need that cooler early. They said you'd have it."

I remember looking at the cooler on the passenger seat. There were several labels on the outside sleeve, but the patient's name he had said was not visible from where he stood.

"Who are you?" I asked.

He looked past me toward the passenger seat.

"It's the one for her," he said. Then he named the medication written beside that patient's entry on the manifest at Clinic A.

That was when I went completely still.

One hand was on the steering wheel. The other was resting near the window switch. My eyes went from his face to the cooler and then to the door lock. I remember noticing my thumb was pressed against the edge of the switch hard enough to hurt.

For half a second I thought he could be somebody from the clinic who had been told about the delivery. Then he said, "You saw the sheet back there. It's the same name."

I had not told him I had seen any sheet.

The open manifest was inside Clinic A.

He was outside my van at Clinic B.

I understood the problem all at once. He wasn't asking for whichever cooler happened to be nearest. He had named one patient and the medication written beside that name. He had already read the manifest, and he knew which delivery he wanted me to hand over.

I hit the lock button.

He looked down at the sound.

I raised the window the rest of the way.

He put his hand on the glass once, not hard. Then he pointed toward the passenger seat and mouthed something I could not hear.

I shook my head and started moving.

He walked with the van for a few steps.

I had the wheel turned toward the exit, so the driver's side passed close to him before I could straighten out. He reached across the gap toward the open space near the mirror. His fingers touched the edge of the door frame while the window was still finishing its last inch upward.

Then he grabbed the driver's door handle.

The handle lifted.

The door stayed shut because I had locked it a moment earlier.

He pulled again.

I pressed the accelerator.

His hand slipped from the handle, but he moved alongside the van and reached toward the passenger side through the space created as I turned. The cooler was sitting on the seat where I always kept it, close enough that somebody at an open door could have grabbed it.

I reached across and shoved the cooler down onto the floor.

That made the van drift toward the edge of the gravel. I corrected too hard and felt the tires bite when I got back onto the packed part of the lot.

In the mirror, he was already turning around.

He did not chase the van on foot.

He went back toward the sedan.

I did not wait to see whether he got inside.

The road out of Clinic B was narrow for the first stretch. I kept expecting headlights to appear behind me. My hands wanted to keep checking the mirrors, but I made myself look forward because there were drainage ditches on both sides and no shoulder worth using.

I tried my phone while I drove. No signal.

That was the part I hated most afterward, the few minutes between the clinic and the highway. I knew there was a person behind me who had tried my door. I knew he wanted a specific cooler. I also knew that if my van quit or I slid off the road, I could not call anybody from where I was.

I had liked how empty that route felt right up until emptiness meant there was nobody close enough to help.

A pair of headlights appeared behind me near the last bend.

I couldn't tell if they belonged to the sedan. I did not slow down to find out.

The pharmacy hub came into view when I reached the highway. It was a low building with a loading area on one side and bright security lights across the front. Trucks came and went through the night, so there were usually people inside even when the public doors were locked.

I turned into the lot and drove straight toward the lit entrance.

The headlights behind me continued past on the highway.

I parked close to the building and locked the van again before I touched anything else.

My phone picked up service.

I called 911 from inside the locked van.

I told the dispatcher I was a medical courier, that a man had followed me into an unstaffed clinic lot, asked for a specific delivery by patient name, and pulled my driver's door handle when I tried to leave. I gave the sedan description as best I could. I did not have a plate.

The dispatcher told me to stay where I was.

I called my supervisor next. That call was shorter. I said I was safe at the pharmacy hub and that the Clinic B delivery had not been completed. My supervisor said to leave the cooler sealed and wait for police.

I stayed in the van with the doors locked.

People were moving inside the pharmacy hub. I could see them through the glass, but I didn't go in because the dispatcher knew where my van was and I did not want to miss the deputy.

The sedan never came into the lot.

A deputy arrived and parked beside me. I got out only after he came to my window and identified himself.

He took my report there under the lights.

I told him about Clinic A first. I told him the back door had been propped open and that the route manifest was visible through the gap. I told him I had called the clinic's after-hours number and left a message. Then I explained the car behind me, the dark parking lot at Clinic B, the patient name, the medication, and the hand on my door handle.

He asked whether the man had shown identification.

I said no.

He asked if I had ever seen him before.

I said no.

He asked whether anything had been taken.

I looked down at the cooler on the floor of the passenger side and said no.

He wrote all of it down.

When I gave him the sedan description, he checked what I could provide and said no vehicle matching that description was registered nearby. He did not say that meant anything. I had no plate, no second witness, and nothing that could prove the car he checked was the one I had seen.

He finished taking my report and left me at the pharmacy hub.

I stayed there until my supervisor arranged for someone else to take the sealed cooler. I did not drive back to Clinic B that night.

Later, while I was filling out the incident paperwork from memory, I kept coming back to the man's wording. He had not only known a patient name. He had said I had seen the sheet back there.

The patient name he used was on a manifest sheet left open at the first clinic, meaning he had been inside before I ever arrived.

That part bothered me more than the door handle. If he had only wanted a cooler, there were easier ways to approach me. If he had only followed the van from the road, he could not have known what was written inside Clinic A. He had connected the open building, my route, and one exact delivery before he ever walked up to my window.

I went back to regular daytime work for a while. When I started taking overnight routes again, I changed one small thing. I stopped doing paperwork in dark clinic lots. If anything felt wrong, I drove to the pharmacy hub first and handled calls under those lights.

My van still ends those overnight runs under the pharmacy lights, because no one ever explained how he knew my route stopped at Clinic B before I got there, or why the manifest at Clinic A identified the exact cooler beside me that night.
